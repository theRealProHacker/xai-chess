# Grading the Reasons, Not Just the Moves: Causal-Intervention Evaluation of Chess Move-Explanations and Chess as a Ground-Truth Substrate — A Survey

*A survey compiled from the xai-chess research corpus (64 works). 2026-06-27.*

## Abstract

Move-explanations from chess engines and language-model tutors read as authoritative, yet the field largely grades them by how fluent, plausible, or satisfying they are to a human reader, rather than by whether the cited reason actually drove the decision. This is a hybrid work—a survey and position paper that synthesizes 64 works and also introduces an embedded benchmark with the authors' own results, kept rhetorically separate from the surveyed evidence throughout. The faithfulness-evaluation community has converged, by independent reinvention, on a single remedy—the intervention-consistency recipe: neutralize a cited factor against a matched baseline and test statistically whether the decision moves. That recipe is mature but bottlenecked on ground truth, with the best hand-curated metric stalling near 0.70 AUROC. This survey reads 64 works across eight thematic clusters—spanning faithfulness evaluation, chess interpretability and planning, and game-AI explanation—through one causal question, and argues that two ground-truth targets are routinely conflated: explainer-internal faithfulness (whether an explanation reflects the explainer's own computation) versus decision-level causal load-bearing (whether a cited factor actually changes a competent decision). Chess does not solve the first, but supplies the second verifiably, because factor presence is rule-decidable, decision quality is read from a strong oracle, and the same factor can be suspended and re-scored. The survey synthesizes this into the xai-chess benchmark, whose scoped novelty is a confound-free rule-level intervention surface plus a measurable agreement between two intervention surfaces graded against an external oracle—differenced from prior non-text state-level causal interventions [32] and remove-piece chess attribution [19], rather than claimed as first by non-text-ness alone. Its headline finding—reported here as the authors' own result, reproducible from the project repository and pending external validation, not as third-party surveyed evidence—is that surface choice determines whether the metric works: the confound-free rule-level surface separates load-bearing factors from red herrings far better (AUROC 0.82) than board-level (0.69) or saliency (0.61) baselines, clearing a reference bar the others miss. The contribution is a validated substrate and a grounding property complementary to, not a replacement for, internal faithfulness.

**Keywords:** explanation faithfulness; intervention-consistency; causal load-bearing; counterfactual evaluation; chess interpretability; oracle engine; explainable AI

## 1. Introduction

A chess engine or a language-model tutor can produce a fluent, expert-sounding reason for any move it recommends. "The knight dominates because it cannot be driven away," or "this wins because of the pin on the f-file." The prose reads as authoritative, and even a strong player will often nod along. Modern explainers are good at this precisely because fluency and plausibility are what they are trained and selected to produce. Yet most of the field grades such explanations by how fluent, plausible, or satisfying they are to a human reader, not by whether the cited reason actually drove the decision. Jacovi and Goldberg [30] named this conflation precisely: a plausible explanation convinces a person, whereas a faithful one reflects the model's true reasoning process, and the two routinely come apart. The distinction matters most exactly where explanations are used to teach, to justify, or to build trust. An explanation can name a factor that did no causal work whatsoever, and nothing in the prose lets a reader tell the difference.

The faithfulness-evaluation community has settled on a remedy. Neutralize the cited factor with a matched baseline, then statistically test whether the model's decision moves. ICE [17] gives this recipe its most complete statistical form (matched-random baselines, a win rate against the null, effect sizes, bootstrap intervals, and multiple-comparison control), and reports that measured faithfulness is strongly operator-dependent and essentially uncorrelated with human plausibility. RFEval [16] reaches the same separation from a different direction, intervening on a model's stated reasoning to show that accuracy is no proxy for faithfulness. That several independent teams converge on the same neutralize-and-retest recipe—Lanham [29], RFEval [16], and ICE [17], with Turpin [28] as the input-side proto-instance—marks it as shared field property rather than any one group's invention, while a second tier of meta-evaluators ([14], [15], [44]) reuses the same logic one level up, to grade the metrics themselves. The method is mature. What it lacks is ground truth. BonaFide [14] builds verifiable labels at considerable cost and finds that existing metrics still hover near chance. Causal Diagnosticity [15] manufactures edit-based labels that the model under test often ignores, leaving the "ground truth" unconfirmed. The recipe is settled, but the labels are missing.

Validating such a metric requires a substrate with a particular property. One must be able to neutralize a factor, re-measure a competent decision, and decide both the factor's presence and the decision's quality independently of the metric being tested. Free text rarely offers this, because the reasoning that produced an answer is itself unobservable and the answer's quality is often a matter of opinion. Chess offers all three at once. Whether a factor such as an absolute pin is present is decidable from the rules, the quality of a move is read from a strong oracle engine, and the same factor can be suspended so the decision is re-scored. The chess-interpretability literature has circled this opening without taking it. McGrath et al. [1] recovered human concepts inside AlphaZero with high fidelity but stressed that probing is correlational, and flagged the pin / can_capture_queen confound that only an intervention can resolve. The nearest existing attribution method, SHAP-for-chess [19], already removes pieces and reads the engine's evaluation delta, yet it attributes static board value with no causal-faithfulness framing and no confound-free intervention surface. A rule-governed substrate also admits an intervention that text cannot imitate: suspending the rule that creates a factor, rather than moving the pieces around it, which lets the same claim be tested twice and the two readings compared.

This survey reads roughly 64 works across faithfulness evaluation, chess interpretability and planning, and game-AI explanation through one question, and argues a single through-line: the evaluation method is settled, the binding constraint is ground truth, two ground-truth targets must not be conflated, and chess is the substrate that supplies the verifiable one. The two targets are explainer-internal faithfulness, meaning whether an explanation reflects the explainer's own internal computation, and decision-level causal load-bearing, meaning whether a cited factor actually changes a competent decision. Chess does not solve the first, because a graded commentator keeps its own unobservable internals. It does supply the second, verifiably, by intervening on the board or on the rules and reading an oracle's valuation. Collapsing the two targets is the field's central category confusion, and keeping them apart forces a lexical discipline the survey holds throughout: the word faithfulness names the first target only, and is never claimed for the decision-level property the chess substrate measures. The benchmark this survey introduces is named xai-chess (its earlier working name was ChessFaith, retired precisely because "faithfulness" is the one property it does not claim to measure). It is positioned here not as "first" by non-text-ness alone—prior work already intervenes causally on non-text substrates [32], [19]—but as a scoped contribution whose novelty is a confound-free rule-level intervention surface together with a measurable agreement between intervention surfaces graded against a separate oracle, differenced carefully against those nearest neighbors in Section 11.

The contributions are four. First, a unifying reading lens that joins three previously separate literatures under one causal question. Second, a tiered, IEEE-numbered map of 64 works organized into eight thematic clusters. Third, an explicit distinction between the two ground-truth targets that the field's loose use of "faithfulness" silently conflates, together with the lexical discipline that distinction demands. Fourth, the positioning of a confound-free, oracle-backed intervention-consistency test with measurable multi-surface agreement, differenced carefully against its nearest prior art in non-text causal intervention [32] and in chess attribution [19]. Throughout, the claim is kept deliberately narrow: the survey shows where a verifiable causal test becomes possible, not that such a test dissolves the harder problem of reading an explainer's own internals.

The remainder is organized in three movements that funnel into a synthesis. Movement I (Sections 3–4) surveys how the field defines and measures faithfulness, and why both of its evaluation traditions stall at proxies. Movement II (Sections 5–7) surveys what chess networks represent, how they plan, and which engines can serve as oracles, establishing that chess carries verifiable decision-level causal structure. Movement III (Sections 8–10) surveys the systems that actually emit chess and game explanations to be graded. Section 11 fuses the movements into the synthesis, Section 12 abstracts field-wide limitations, and Section 13 concludes. Before any of that, Section 2 fixes the survey's own scope and method.

## 2. Scope & Method of This Survey

The survey covers three intersecting bodies of work. The first is explanation-faithfulness and causal-evaluation methodology, drawn mainly from NLP, large-model, and vision XAI. The second is chess and board-game interpretability: concept probing, look-ahead and planning circuits, and human-aligned engines. The third is game-AI explanation and commentary, in chess and at its boundary in other games. The time span runs from roughly 2013 to 2026, and the modality span runs from early vision and NLP attribution, through chess-specific networks, to present-day language models. A small set of cross-domain works at the edge of scope, including maze-solving world models, explainable reinforcement learning, and card-game commentary, is retained where it bears on whether the intervention-consistency idea generalizes beyond chess. Work whose only contribution is engine playing strength, with no interpretability or explanation angle and no path to a causal-intervention reading, falls outside the scope.

The corpus was assembled in three passes. It began with the reference list of an original research proposal for the benchmark. It was broadened by an office-hours and landscape scan that pulled in adjacent faithfulness-evaluation and chess-interpretability work the proposal had not cited. It was closed by a completeness-verification pass that filled the remaining gaps and pruned redundancy. The result is 64 works, numbered [1]–[64] in IEEE order and cited by those fixed numbers throughout. The numbering is held constant so that a reference doubles as a stable tag for the subsection that treats it, letting the reader move between the synthesis and a paper's full treatment without re-resolving its identity.

Each work carries a relevance tier set by how load-bearing it is to an intervention-grounded benchmark: 23 Core, 33 Supporting, and 8 Peripheral. Core works are the methods, oracles, explanation engines, and faithfulness machinery the survey's lens engages directly. Supporting works are methodologically adjacent: interpretability, simulatability, concept-evaluation, and search-explanation studies that inform the design without being built upon. Peripheral works supply broader game-AI and human-move-modeling context. The tiers express distance from the causal-intervention question that organizes the survey, not a judgment of any work's quality or importance in its own field.

The lens is a single question asked of every work: does it verify that a cited factor is causally load-bearing for a decision, or does it stop short of that test? Four stop-points recur across the corpus. Correlational probing establishes that a concept is decodable but never that the network uses it to decide. Simulatability and plausibility measure whether an explanation convinces a human or helps one predict behavior, which is a separate axis from faithfulness. Unverified-edit labels construct a counterfactual but never confirm the model acted on it. Behavioral move-match reproduces the moves a human would play with no account of why. The same lens governs the verdict the survey withholds: a work is never faulted for failing to reach a causal test it did not set out to run, only placed by where its evidence halts relative to that test. Naming the four stop-points is what makes the survey falsifiable, because each work can then be located precisely rather than praised or dismissed in the abstract.

The 64 works sort into eight thematic clusters, mapped one-to-one onto the survey's chapters, with every paper appearing exactly once. In the order the chapters take them, the clusters are faithfulness and causal evaluation of explanations (the method ancestors), simulatability and concept-evaluation pitfalls, concept knowledge and probing in chess models, look-ahead and internal planning circuits, chess engines and human-aligned models, chess explanation and grounded commentary, explaining search and MCTS, and game-AI explanation beyond chess.

The genre should be stated plainly, because it is hybrid: this is at once a narrative, lens-driven survey, a position paper that argues a specific reading of the field, and the home of an embedded benchmark whose results are the authors' own. It is not a PRISMA-style systematic review—there was no pre-registered protocol, no exhaustive database query, and no quantitative screening flow. Coverage is guided by relevance to a single causal question rather than by reproducible enumeration of a defined search space. The two registers are kept apart throughout: every chapter from 3 to 10 reports only surveyed third-party evidence, and Section 11 is the sole chapter licensed to report the authors' own xai-chess results, flagged as such and as pending external validation wherever they appear. The narrative form suits the goal, which is to trace one argument across literatures that rarely cite one another, not to certify exhaustive retrieval within a single field. The aim is synthesis, and the next chapter opens Movement I with how the field defines and tries to measure faithfulness.

## 3. Faithfulness and Causal Evaluation of Explanations

This lineage does two things the rest of the survey depends on. It fixes what *faithfulness* means and how the field agreed to test it, and it then turns those instruments back on themselves, asking whether a faithfulness metric can be trusted at all. Both moves converge on a single obstacle. The recipe the community settled on is mature and statistically disciplined, yet every attempt to validate it founders for want of labels that state, independently, whether a cited reason actually did the work.

The field's working definition is owed to Jacovi and Goldberg [30], who separate two properties that interpretability research had been conflating: faithfulness, how accurately an explanation reflects the model's true reasoning process, and plausibility, how convincing that explanation looks to a human reader. Their account is conceptual rather than empirical. It names three assumptions that underlie existing faithfulness tests (that two models predicting alike reason alike, that similar inputs are decided by similar reasoning, and that input parts contribute importance independently), and shows each is used mainly to *disprove* a faithfulness claim by counterexample rather than to certify one. The guidelines that follow are largely prohibitions: do not use human judgment or gold labels to assess faithfulness, because those measure plausibility, and distrust both claims of inherent interpretability and the use of end-task utility as a proxy. Jacovi and Goldberg also abandon the binary faithful/unfaithful verdict, proposing instead a graded notion conditioned on task, model, and input region. The paper supplies no metric, and that silence is the seed of the chapter's problem. If a model's true reasoning is unobservable and human judgment is ruled out, positive verification has nothing left to anchor to.

The first empirical demonstrations that the gap matters came from probing chain-of-thought (CoT) traces and finding the stated reasons were often not the operative ones. Turpin et al. [28] inject biasing features that ought to be irrelevant, such as a few-shot reordering that always makes answer "(A)" correct, or a stereotype-aligned cue, then check whether the model's CoT ever admits to using them. Across thirteen BIG-Bench Hard tasks, the planted bias lowers accuracy by as much as 36%, while the generated reasoning almost never names the bias and instead manufactures a plausible-sounding rationalization for the biased answer. The lesson is sharp: a fluent, plausible explanation is no evidence that its cited reason was load-bearing, so faithfulness has to be probed by intervention rather than read off the text. The study's own reach is narrow, since unfaithfulness is operationalized as the failure to verbalize a *known* planted bias, a coarse signal that detects omission but cannot quantify how much any cited factor drove the output.

Lanham et al. [29] generalize the intervention from the input to the reasoning trace itself, and in doing so supply the proto-recipe the field would standardize. They define faithfulness through four perturbations and the resulting answer changes: truncating the CoT early to see whether the answer was already fixed, injecting a corrupted step to see whether the model follows the error, paraphrasing to rule out steganographic phrase-encoding, and replacing the trace with filler tokens to rule out mere added compute. Answer-change rates span a wide range, from 44% on AQuA (highly faithful) to about 2% on ARC Easy (effectively post-hoc), and faithfulness falls as model size rises on most tasks. The shape of the method is exactly the one that recurs hereafter: perturb a candidate cause, hold everything else fixed, and measure whether the decision moves. Its limitation is equally telling, because faithfulness is inferred purely from behavioral sensitivity, with no external check on whether the cited content is genuinely decision-relevant.

These threads converge on what can be called the intervention-consistency recipe: neutralize a cited factor against a matched baseline and statistically test whether the model's decision shifts. ICE [17] is its most complete statistical expression. Its core quantity, normalized score retention, is compared against matched random-rationale baselines through a randomization test, and the framework reports a primary win rate, Cohen's *d* against the random null, bootstrap confidence intervals, conservative one-sided p-values, and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control, under two intervention operators. The findings vindicate Jacovi and Goldberg empirically: faithfulness is strongly operator-dependent, with win-rate gaps up to 44 percentage points, nearly a third of configurations score below their random baselines, and faithfulness is essentially uncorrelated with human plausibility (|r| < 0.04). RFEval [16] reaches the same recipe from the reasoning-model side, covering 7,186 instances across seven domains and twelve open large reasoning models. It defines faithfulness through stance consistency and a causal-influence test under output-level counterfactual interventions, judges 49.7% of outputs unfaithful, and reports that once task and model are controlled, accuracy is neither a sufficient nor a reliable proxy for faithfulness; it adds that reinforcement-style fine-tuning can degrade faithfulness while leaving accuracy intact. Both works share the recipe and both stop at the same wall. ICE measures behavioral sensitivity to perturbations with no oracle, so a high win rate certifies only that the attribution tracks *this* model, never that the cited factor is genuinely load-bearing in a correct decision. RFEval infers causal influence from the model's own behavior, with a single deterministic verdict and no randomization layer to fix a null distribution against which "no effect" could be read.

The deepest version of the difficulty surfaces when the field tries to grade the graders. Meta-evaluation asks whether a faithfulness metric can separate faithful from unfaithful explanations, and that question needs items whose true faithfulness is already known. BonaFide [14] builds such ground truth at considerable cost: 3,066 labeled CoTs across thirteen tasks and ten models, using an "outright" design where a correct answer entails that a specific bottleneck computation ran, and a "diversionary" design where steps that acknowledge or conceal a misleading hint are labeled accordingly. Eight metrics across four families are scored against these labels, and the result is bleak: most sit near chance, several show strong prediction biases and degrade on longer traces, none transfers across the two settings, and the best CoT-level metric reaches only AUROC 0.70 while the best step-level metric reaches 0.59. That ceiling is informative precisely because the labels were curated by hand, and the metrics that approach it are the most computationally expensive of the suite. The bottleneck is the labels, not the arithmetic, and even those labels exist only on synthetic, structured tasks where "which computation produced the answer" is externally inferable. Causal Diagnosticity [15] tries to manufacture labels more cheaply, editing a model into two variants that return the same answer for different reasons so that each variant's self-explanation is faithful to itself and unfaithful to the other. A metric's diagnosticity is then the fraction of such pairs on which it scores the faithful explanation higher, with 0.5 the chance baseline. The construction is elegant but the labels go unverified: edit success is gauged by a perplexity ratio, edits succeed on some tasks and fall below 50% reliability on others, and the models frequently do not reflect the injected knowledge at all, forcing the authors onto synthetic explanations to keep the labels valid for the main results. BonaFide flags exactly this, faulting edit-based ground truth for never confirming that the model used the edited knowledge.

When verified labels cannot be produced, the field improvises around their absence, and the two standard improvisations both leave a proxy in the label's place. ICE [17] substitutes randomized matched baselines precisely because per-item faithfulness labels are unavailable, so its verdict is comparative across operators rather than absolute. BUMP [44] constructs a substrate instead, having annotators inject a single controlled error into a faithful reference summary to yield roughly 889 unfaithful minimal pairs, then meta-evaluates twelve metrics on consistency and discriminability. The minimal-pair design is genuinely harder than model-generated benchmarks and exposes that the most discriminative metric (about 71.5% AUC) is not the most consistent, but the labels remain a construction: human-injected single edits rather than naturally occurring hallucinations, requiring roughly 16% manual correction, with no causal-intervention notion of faithfulness behind them.

Taken together, this chapter establishes that the method is settled and the bottleneck is ground truth. Independent teams have repeatedly rediscovered the same neutralize-and-retest-with-statistics pattern as a recipe [29], [16], [17] (with Turpin [28] as the input-side proto-instance), which is strong evidence that the recipe is now shared field property rather than anyone's novelty; a second tier of meta-evaluators operates one level up, grading the metrics themselves rather than instantiating the recipe [14], [15], [44]. One feature unites every instrument in this chapter and matters for the synthesis: each intervenes on the explainer's own reasoning or inputs and reads the explainer's own decision, so each estimates explainer-internal self-faithfulness—the first ground-truth target. What none of them can supply is a label that says, on its own authority, whether a cited factor was causally load-bearing for a competent decision. The label-having efforts top out near 0.70 under costly hand curation [14] or rest on unverified edits [15]; the label-lacking efforts randomize their baselines [17] or build minimal pairs by hand [44]. Each is a proxy standing in for a causal fact that text cannot make decidable. What is missing is a substrate where that fact is settled by construction rather than inferred from behavior, where the presence of a factor is decidable from the rules, a factor can be neutralized while everything else is held fixed, and a strong, factor-independent valuation can be re-read to see whether the decision actually depended on it. No quantity of additional metric engineering closes that gap, because the limit is evidential rather than statistical. Such a substrate does not exist in free text, where the very question of which computation produced an answer is often ill-posed, but it can exist outside natural language. The remainder of this survey argues that chess supplies exactly this: a domain in which a cited factor's contribution to a competent decision is verifiable rather than assumed, and against which the intervention-consistency recipe assembled here can finally be checked.

## 4. Simulatability, concept-based evaluation, and the pitfalls of XAI metrics

The intervention-consistency lineage of Chapter 3 tests the model side of an explanation: neutralize a cited factor and watch whether the decision moves. A parallel tradition tests the human (or human-proxy) side, asking whether an explanation lets an observer anticipate what the model will do next. This simulatability and forward-prediction school, together with its concept-based and reinforcement-learning offshoots, has produced the field's most sobering null results and its sharpest warnings about which evaluation signals can be trusted. Read through this survey's lens, every method here stops at a behavioral or subjective proxy rather than verifying that a cited factor is causally load-bearing for a decision, and several show that the proxies the community leans on hardest are close to worthless.

The vocabulary for this debate comes from Doshi-Velez and Kim's agenda for a rigorous science of interpretability [24]. They argue that interpretability is a means to auxiliary ends (safety, trust, transferability, the ability to audit for unwanted causality) that accuracy alone cannot certify, and that where those needs are absent or already formalizable, interpretability may be unnecessary. Their organizing device is a three-tier evaluation taxonomy ordered by cost and validity: application-grounded (real users on real tasks), human-grounded (real users on simplified proxy tasks), and functionally-grounded (no humans, a formal proxy metric pre-validated against human studies). Their lasting contribution is normative: interpretability claims should be treated as hypotheses that demand tier-matched evidence, not asserted from intuition. The agenda nonetheless centers human understanding and never specifies how to build a functionally-grounded proxy that certifies an explanation reflects the model's actual decision mechanism, leaving the very metric this survey's lens demands as an open call.

Hase and Bansal supply the first systematic attempt to answer that call behaviorally [25]. Across tabular and text classification they ran controlled human-subject studies under two protocols, forward simulation (predict the model's output on unseen inputs after studying explained examples) and counterfactual simulation (predict the response to perturbed inputs), comparing LIME, Anchors, decision-boundary, prototype, and composite explanations against a no-explanation control. Effectiveness proved rare and method-specific: LIME helped for tabular data, the prototype method helped on counterfactuals, and most conditions showed no clear benefit at all. The decisive observation for this survey is that users' subjective ratings of explanation quality did not predict how much an explanation actually improved their predictions. Perceived value and functional value came apart, and self-report failed as a signal. The protocol still conflates whether an explanation reveals true model reasoning with whether a user can exploit it, so a faithful explanation could fail the test and an unfaithful one could pass.

Chen et al. sharpen the simulatability idea into counterfactual simulatability for natural-language explanations [18]. A good explanation, they argue, should let a person predict the model's output on related, modified inputs; they prompt GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to generate diverse counterfactuals per instance and score simulation precision and generality via human entailment judgments. Both chain-of-thought and post-hoc explanations reached only moderate precision, and precision was essentially uncorrelated with human-rated plausibility (Pearson near 0.012), which the authors read as direct evidence that optimizing for human approval, as reinforcement learning from human feedback does, fails to produce explanations that track behavior. The method's own ground truth is the same model's possibly inconsistent output rather than any external fact, so it measures behavioral self-consistency rather than whether a cited reason did causal work.

Two follow-ups stress the same machinery and reach the same boundary. Limpijankit et al. extend counterfactual simulatability from yes/no QA to open-ended generation, decomposing an explanation into atomic units and checking whether those units reappear in the model's outputs on generated counterfactuals [50]. Explanations predicted behavior well for skill-based summarization but poorly for knowledge-based medical suggestion, and the metric remains a self-referential text-match with no external check that a cited factor caused the behavior. Hong and Roth instead tighten the counterfactuals, replacing free-form LLM perturbations with theory-driven "pragmatic" ones grounded in Gricean maxims, and test whether self-explanations help LLM judges and 28 human raters predict answers [51]. Explanations did raise prediction accuracy, but the gains were small and shadowed by a much larger jump in rater confidence, and the authors explicitly disclaim any claim about faithfulness to the model's internals. Simulatability, on their own account, measures the predictability of outputs rather than the computation behind them.

ConSim carries the paradigm into concept-based explanation and automates the costly human simulator out of the loop [27]. Poché et al. score concept-extraction methods (among them non-negative matrix factorization, sparse autoencoders, and PCA) by how well an LLM simulator reproduces a classifier's outputs given the extracted concepts and their descriptions, finding method rankings that stay stable across three different LLM simulators. The automation is the appeal and the weakness at once: the authors concede no proof that LLM-simulator rankings match human-simulator rankings, and, more to the point for this survey, simulatability rewards concepts that help predict outputs without establishing that those concepts are causally responsible for the decision. The signal is correlational rather than interventional.

The cautionary high-water mark is ALMANACS [26]. Mills et al. build a fully automated simulatability benchmark in which a GPT-4 predictor, given retrieved examples and an explanation method's output, must anticipate a target model's answers on a deliberately shifted test set designed so that in-context pattern-matching alone cannot succeed. Across twelve safety-relevant topics and four explanation methods (counterfactual, rationalization, attention, integrated gradients), averaged over topics, no method beat the explanation-free control. The result is a standing warning to any simulatability track: absent a positive control proving that a known-good explanation can move the predictor, a null is ambiguous between "explanations do not help" and "the student cannot exploit help." Either reading is damaging, because the most-cited behavioral proxy for explanation usefulness, run at scale, detected no usefulness whatsoever.

The reinforcement-learning side of the field delivers the same verdict about saliency maps, and does so with exactly the move this survey will champion. Atrey, Clary, and Jensen show that saliency maps offered as explanations of deep-RL agents are largely unfalsifiable, citing a survey in which only about 7% of saliency claims were experimentally validated [32]. Their remedy is counterfactual: intervene on the latent game state, using a fully parameterized reimplementation of Atari, rather than on pixels, then test whether intuitive saliency-based hypotheses about the agent survive. None did. Saliency patterns persisted or were confounded by board location even as behavior changed, so the authors recast saliency as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than explanatory of causal structure. The contribution is the template the rest of this survey builds on: a state-level, environment-grounded causal intervention can falsify an explanation that any plausibility check would have waved through.

If satisfaction surveys are the field's softest currency, Shymanski, Brue, and Sen show how little it buys, and they do so in this survey's own chess domain [33]. Their between-subjects study had users learn fork and pin tactics from a chess-teaching agent under three conditions: no explanation, "placebic" explanations ("this move is most advantageous"), and "actionable" explanations naming the tactic. Subjective satisfaction and perceived explanatory power did not differ across conditions, yet objective transfer did, with the actionable group outperforming the placebic group on held-out puzzles. Placebic explanations helped during practice but, unlike actionable ones, left no durable gain at test. Satisfaction, in short, is blind to explanation quality while actionability predicts learning. The outcome measured is human transfer rather than fidelity to the agent's reasoning, so the study convicts satisfaction as a metric without itself certifying that an explanation reflects the model's decision basis.

Two works frame the wider stakes. Cheng et al.'s survey of explainable deep reinforcement learning organizes the field into feature-, state-, dataset-, and model-level explanations and catalogs its evaluation frameworks [59]; its own assessment is that the quantitative metrics in circulation are largely correlational (saliency, perturbation) rather than verifiable interventions, and that coverage of causal, decision-level testing for game-playing agents is thin, which positions a chess-specific causal benchmark as a concrete instantiation of what the field lacks. Riedl and Bogert add the human-side caveat that even a genuinely useful explanation does not help everyone equally [64]. Studying five years of data from over 52,000 online chess players plus dozens of platform-level natural experiments, they find that motivated, stronger players self-select into using AI feedback, that the apparent learning benefit largely vanishes once this selection is controlled, and that centralized AI feedback widens skill gaps and narrows the diversity of play. Whatever an explanation's intrinsic quality, its downstream value is mediated by who receives it.

Taken together, the two evaluation paradigms surveyed so far converge on one diagnosis. The intervention-consistency methods of Chapter 3 are starved of verified labels; the simulatability and concept-evaluation methods of this chapter are well-instrumented yet stall at proxies that repeatedly prove unreliable: plausibility uncorrelated with behavior [18], subjective satisfaction blind to quality [25], [33], automated simulatability unable to beat a no-explanation control [26], and saliency unable to survive a counterfactual test [32]. The recurring lesson is to prefer causal, behavior-grounded evidence over satisfaction or plausibility, and the recurring obstacle is that the substrates studied here, text and pixels, rarely admit a verifiable causal label. Atrey's state-level interventions [32] hint at the way out, a parameterized environment in which the cause can be edited and the effect re-measured. The remainder of the survey asks whether a game can supply that environment in full, beginning with what chess networks internally represent.

## 5. Concept Knowledge & Probing in Chess Models

Chess networks demonstrably encode human chess concepts, and the dominant way to read that knowledge out is concept probing: a classifier or regressor is trained on a layer's activations to predict a human-defined property, and its accuracy is taken as evidence that the property is represented. This chapter surveys that literature as the entry point to Movement II, which asks what chess networks internally know before later chapters ask whether they use it. The readout methods escalate in resolution across the chapter, from single linear probes through learned concept vectors to sparse-autoencoder dictionaries, but the single most consequential feature of the chapter is methodological rather than empirical. Almost every result is correlational. A probe establishes that a concept is decodable from a representation, not that the network relies on it to choose a move, and a high decoding accuracy is silent on whether the network would play differently if the concept were absent. The few studies that do intervene act on the internal activations of one specific network rather than on the decision an external explanation cites. The correlational baseline banked here is exactly what a decision-level causal test is designed to upgrade.

The method ancestor is Testing with Concept Activation Vectors (TCAV) [31], which reframes attribution from individual features to human-named concepts. A user supplies examples of a concept, a linear classifier separates its activations from random counterexamples, and the classifier's normal vector, the concept activation vector, yields a directional sensitivity score for a target class. Scores are filtered through a t-test against many random concept sets so that only statistically reliable directions are reported. TCAV's own validation is pointed: a human study found saliency maps conveyed concept importance barely above chance, which motivated a quantitative alternative. Even so, TCAV measures the correlational sensitivity of an internal representation to a concept, not a verified causal effect on the decision, and it presumes the concept is linearly separable. The chess probing literature inherits both the apparatus and the limitation.

The canonical chess application is McGrath et al.'s study of AlphaZero [1]. Sparse linear probes are trained across the network's layers to recover Stockfish evaluation terms and chess concepts, with what-when-where plots tracking the depth and training step at which each emerges, supplemented by matrix factorization of move-selection factors and commentary from a grandmaster. Material and evaluation probes reach high fidelity, and many concepts appear in a sharp developmental phase, evidence that human-aligned knowledge is genuinely acquired. The authors are unusually explicit about what this does not show: probing measures "only a proximate correlation ... and not causation in any forms," and a probe "cannot tell if we are getting a confound or the concept itself." They name the exact entanglement that motivates the rest of this survey. Under an absolute pin, the concept can_capture_queen cannot be intervened to zero without simultaneously forcing in_check to one, so the two signals move together and no probe can separate their contributions. A correlational readout cannot, even in principle, attribute the network's behavior to one of two factors that always co-occur. Holding the rest of the position fixed while neutralizing a single named factor and re-measuring the decision is the disentanglement that a matched causal intervention, not a probe, provides, and it is the precise operation the absolute-pin confound calls for.

Schut et al. push from reading concepts to discovering and transferring them [2]. Sparse concept vectors are extracted from AlphaZero's latent space, filtered by machine teachability and by novelty relative to a human-game basis, and the survivors are validated in a study where four grandmasters improved after seeing the machine's lines. The result is strong evidence that AlphaZero holds concepts beyond current human theory. For the causal-load-bearing question it stops in the same place McGrath does: a concept is shown to be decodable, teachable, and correlated with strong play, yet the method never neutralizes the concept and re-measures move quality, so it cannot establish that the concept is load-bearing for any particular decision.

Two studies by Hammersborg and Strümke carry the program onto fully open models. The earlier work builds an adaptable, resource-frugal reinforcement-learning chess environment and replicates McGrath-style probing on self-trained agents [41], recovering concepts such as material advantage, queen threats and in_check in an interpretable developmental order and showing that architecture and board geometry shape when each appears; the authors concede they cannot determine how a detected concept influences decisions. The later paper distills Leela Chess Zero into smaller students and adds an information-importance map, a trainable masker that retains only the input a model needs, alongside a probing replication that mirrors AlphaZero's layer-wise concept acquisition on open nets [40]. Both are valuable for reproducibility. Both stop at the same boundary, since the probes show only decodability and the masker's claimed completeness is argued from architecture rather than measured against an external causal oracle.

Lomasov et al. probe whether a grandmaster-level transformer genuinely shares human strategic concepts or only mimics surface patterns [43]. Early layers encode concepts such as center control and knight outposts at up to 85% probe accuracy, but deeper, stronger-playing layers drift toward "alien" encodings that probe far worse, and a new Chess960 concept set shows recognition dropping by 10 to 20 points once opening theory is removed. The reading is that optimizing for strength pushes representations away from human-aligned concepts. The evidence is once more correlational, and the "alien" label is inferred from low probe accuracy, which conflates a genuinely different concept with one that merely lacks linear structure; no move-level test confirms whether any probed concept is actually used.

A smaller line moves past correlation to intervention, and the contrast it draws is instructive. Karvonen trains chess language models on raw game transcripts and recovers the board state per square at up to 99.6% probe accuracy, evidence of an emergent world model rather than surface statistics [3]. The work then goes white-box-causal: deleting a piece from the internal board representation through an activation edit yields consistent downstream play, and a contrastive skill vector added to the activations raises win rate against a weak engine, so the representation drives behavior rather than merely accompanying it. Spies et al. make the analogous move in maze-solving transformers [45], where sparse-autoencoder latents that encode individual maze connections are patched back into the residual stream and toggling them causally edits the solution path. Each study establishes that an internal representation can be causally load-bearing, and together they are the closest internal analog of the external, decision-level edits this survey is built around. Each also marks the limit of the white-box route for the present purpose. The interventions require full model access and a learned probe, they act on a model's own internal state rather than on the decision an external explanation cites, and the property they manipulate is board occupancy or skill rather than a named strategic factor such as a pin. Spies further reports that suppression is the weaker, less reliable direction, which is precisely the direction a "neutralize the factor" test must rely on, a cautionary signal for any benchmark that works by removing a cited factor.

Sparse autoencoders promise a finer decomposition of these representations into interpretable features, and two chess studies show both the appeal and a caution that is central to this survey. Poupart's contrastive sparse autoencoders separate root-board features from planning features in Leela by training on trajectory pairs, surfacing human-recognizable concepts such as piece safety and rook threats [39]. The work is explicitly a proof of concept, its differentiating-feature probe sits barely above chance, and the features are extracted and probed but never ablated to test whether they drive a move. Jiao et al. go further, training sparse autoencoders on Maia-2 and steering identified latents to elicit higher- or lower-skill play and to selectively enhance or sabotage specific tasks [53], a genuine causal manipulation of internal features. The lesson for any benchmark is a binding constraint. The autoencoder features are tied to Maia-2's own representation space and cannot be transplanted or ablated inside a different network such as lc0, so this style of feature ablation is not a portable causal probe. A mechanistic surface of this kind is pinned to one exact network, which is why a model-agnostic intervention on the board or the rules becomes attractive.

A complementary probe of structure comes from out-of-distribution testing. Mészáros et al. train a 270M-parameter chess transformer and stress it with seven distributions that violate the training data, separating rule extrapolation from strategy adaptation [42]. Legal-move accuracy transfers remarkably well, exceeding 99% on out-of-distribution puzzles and in Chess960 play, while strategic quality degrades sharply, and a training-dynamics signature, learning first to "move only your own pieces," is read as emergent compositional structure. The compositionality claim is behavioral rather than mechanistic, with no circuit-level confirmation. The reliable rule-following it documents nonetheless matters here, because it underwrites the premise that a strong engine's response to a rule-level intervention reflects genuine structural reasoning rather than memorized surface frequencies.

This literature establishes that chess networks contain rich, human-recognizable concept structure and that the structure can be read out with growing precision, from linear probes through concept vectors to sparse-autoencoder dictionaries. Its through-line for the survey is the gap it leaves. Most of the evidence is correlational, certifying that a concept is present and decodable rather than that it does causal work, and the studies that do intervene act white-box on one network's activations, inherit a net-binding constraint, and find suppression the unreliable direction. None grade whether a factor cited in an external explanation is causally load-bearing for a competent decision against an oracle, the question on which a decision-level test turns. That gap, together with the absolute-pin confound McGrath names, is the premise this chapter deposits for the synthesis. Whether chess networks actually use these concepts to plan is the question the next chapter takes up.

## 6. Look-ahead, planning, and internal circuits

Chess policy networks compute information about future moves and use it to choose the present one, which makes planning in these models a causally testable phenomenon rather than a behavioral inference. This chapter banks two facts the Synthesis later spends. First, the substrate carries rule-grounded internal causal structure that interventions can confirm, evidence that decision-level causal facts about chess are real and decidable. Second, that internal structure does not determine output behavior: a line can be computed inside the network and then discarded before it reaches the move. The second fact is what licenses grading explanations against an oracle's decision rather than against its internals. One caveat frames the whole literature: every result below is pinned to a specific network checkpoint, so the mechanisms are net-specific rather than generic properties of chess models.

Jenner et al. [4] supply the causal-ground-truth template. Studying Leela Chess Zero's T82 transformer on a curated set of forcing tactical puzzles, they combine three methods: activation patching, attention-head analysis, and a bilinear probe. Patching the square of the move three plies in the future drops the log-odds of the correct present move by 1.88, against at most 0.55 for alternative squares, and the probe reads off the move two turns ahead at 92% accuracy where a randomly initialized network scores 15%. The network therefore represents and uses optimal future moves, not merely the current board. The evidence stops short of behavior over real games and of verified multi-step planning: it is confined to a sacrifice-heavy, single-best-line subset, and pattern-matching correlated with future squares is hard to exclude entirely. For this survey the work matters as the internal analogue of an external intervention — show a feature is load-bearing for the engine's decision by neutralizing it and re-measuring.

Cruz [23] extends the look-ahead account and qualifies its reach. Using the same Leela variant and the same toolkit of patching, probing, and head ablation, the study finds look-ahead to be strongly position-dependent rather than universal, with future-move information decodable up to roughly seven plies, decaying but staying above chance. The branch-corruption experiments are the most telling: in near-equiprobable positions, corrupting one branch's third-move square shifts the network's choice to the alternative branch, evidence of simultaneous multi-line consideration. The author concedes that the method cannot separate genuine planning from sophisticated pattern matching, and the analysis is largely per-puzzle, without dataset-wide effect sizes or significance tests. Probing here registers decodability, which is not the same as causal use — a distinction the searchless-transformer work below sharpens.

The complication central to the survey's argument comes from Sandmann et al. [5], who bind to the same Leela T82 family. Applying a logit-lens read of the policy head layer by layer, they rate each layer's move distribution by an internal tournament Elo and find strength rising near-monotonically with depth. The decisive observation concerns where correct moves go: tactical solutions, including immediate mates, often surface in intermediate layers and are then systematically overridden by late layers that prefer safe play to aggression — the "forgotten puzzles." Look-ahead is intact and causally important throughout, so the loss is a preference conflict, not an algorithmic failure; steering against the safety bias recovers most of the forgotten solutions. The implication for evaluation is direct. If a solution can be found internally and discarded before output, then an evaluation that grades internal computation and one that grades emitted behavior are answering different questions. Grading against the oracle's decision is well-posed because the decision is observable and definite, whereas grading against its computation is not, because the computation may never reach the output. This is the mechanism behind the survey's insistence that decision-level causal load-bearing and explainer-internal faithfulness are distinct targets.

Circuit-level tracing gives the most granular view of that internal structure. Lin et al. [6] decompose both the MLP and attention modules of LC0 BT4, a search-free grandmaster-level transformer, using Top-K transcoders for the feed-forward layers and low-rank sparse attention to split heads into interpretable features. The resulting features are validated directly against the rules of chess (piece detection, tactical threats such as pins and forks, defensive relations) at high precision, and feature steering quantifies their causal effect on the output. A case study makes the payoff concrete: ablating a single defensive over-evaluation feature raises a missed forced-mate move from 28.8% to 90.6%, exposing the blind spot mechanistically. The causal claims rest on a few hand-picked positions without matched controls or significance testing, and the target is the network's internal features, not the faithfulness of any natural-language explanation. As an anchor it shows what a load-bearing factor such as a pin looks like inside a strong engine, complementing the decision-level test at the heart of this survey.

Two studies recast searchless transformers as amortized planners and, in doing so, supply a usable oracle. Ruoss et al. [7] train decoder-only transformers to play chess by distilling Stockfish 16 with no search at inference; the largest action-value model reaches grandmaster-level blitz strength searchlessly, and its action-value head emits a calibrated per-move win probability. The authors read this as amortized planning, a feedforward network internalizing the result of a search it never runs. For an intervention benchmark the action-value head is attractive on its own terms: it is a search-free, metric-independent valuation function that can serve directly as the oracle's win-probability estimate, sidestepping centipawn-to-win conversion. Its limits are inherited: the model distills Stockfish and cannot exceed its teacher, and it offers no account of why a move is good. Li and Jiang [52] then factor playing strength into state tracking, reconstructing the board from move history, and decision quality, picking strong moves, formalizing a bottleneck in which overall play is capped by the weaker of the two. They validate tracking with linear board-state probes over hundreds of thousands of positions, but flag that the probes establish decodability without demonstrating causal use. That gap is the survey's argument in miniature: confirming a cited factor is causally load-bearing requires a decision-level intervention rather than a probe that merely shows the information is present. Net binding separates these results from the Leela work above (Ruoss's is a distinct action-value transformer, Li and Jiang's a next-token model with no board input), reinforcing that each mechanistic surface is tied to its own checkpoint.

Beyond chess, Singh et al. [46] anchor the planning-explanation problem in model-based reinforcement learning. Their method explains a deep RL agent's decisions with a Reverse World Model that generates the prior states under which the agent would have preferred a counterfactual action, answering not which alternative the agent rejected but what the world would have had to be. A human study finds that users given these explanations identify the cause of agent failure far more accurately than a control group. The validation is the telling point: the explanations are judged by human understanding and trust rather than by any objective measure that the generated states reflect the agent's actual decision mechanism, so a plausible-looking artifact would pass. That contrast is what an oracle-graded, intervention-based benchmark is built to avoid. The entry sits closer to the simulatability and human-prediction tradition surveyed earlier than to mechanistic chess planning, and is included here as the cross-domain planning-explanation anchor.

Taken together, the chapter deposits three premises. Chess networks plan and carry rule-grounded, causally testable internal structure [4], [6], [23], so verifiable decision-level causal facts about the substrate exist. Internal computation and emitted behavior diverge [5], so the well-posed target for grading an explanation is the decision, not the computation. And searchless value heads function as amortized planners [7], [52], supplying a calibrated win-probability oracle to grade against. Each surface is net-specific, a fragility the field-limitations chapter returns to. These are the facts the Synthesis spends when it argues chess can validate an intervention-consistency metric against ground truth that text cannot supply.

## 7. Chess engines and human-aligned models

Engines enter this survey in two roles. As an oracle, a strong engine supplies the metric-independent valuation an intervention is measured against: neutralize a cited factor, re-query the engine, and read the change in its assessment. As the system under study, an engine is the move source whose choice an explanation purports to account for. Two families fill these roles. Superhuman search engines, including Stockfish and the AlphaZero/Leela line, maximize playing strength and furnish the oracle; human-aligned imitation models reproduce the move a player of a stated strength would make, and supply the move-source baselines and the difficulty calibration that decide whom an explanation is for. This chapter surveys the second family and the move-prediction and skill-modeling work around it, reading each through the survey's lens: does any verify that a cited factor is causally load-bearing for a decision, or does it stop at predicting which move a human plays?

The Maia line established chess as a model system for human-AI alignment by retraining an AlphaZero-style policy network to predict human moves rather than the strongest one [20]. McIlroy-Young et al. fit nine separate residual networks, one per 100-point Elo band from 1100 to 1900, and each matched human play best at its own target rating, reaching above 52% move-matching where Stockfish managed only 33-41% [20]. The result reframed alignment as a problem of modeling human errors rather than scaling strength, and a companion network even forecasts where a given rating will blunder, at accuracies in the high sixties to high seventies of percent [20]. Yet the model predicts which move a human plays and gives no account of why; its per-bin design is behavioral, never intervenes on the board to test a cause, and degrades above its 1900 training ceiling for want of stronger data [20]. Maia-2 folded the rating bins into one skill-conditioned network, injecting skill-level embeddings into the attention mechanism so a single model spans the spectrum, and it produced far more coherent monotonic skill scaling and lower perplexity than its predecessor [9]. Its heads are policy, value, and auxiliary only: Maia-2 has no natural-language channel and can therefore neither read nor emit an explanation, which confines its use to that of a human-difficulty calibrator that maps a position to a per-rating move-match probability [9].

Chessformer carries the lineage to a unified encoder-only transformer that treats skill as a continuous axis [8]. A learnable rating soft-embedding interpolates between a weak anchor and a strong anchor, so one architecture instantiates both a human-emulation family, Maia-3, and a strength configuration folded into Leela Chess Zero that raises the base engine's playing strength [8]. Maia-3 reports higher move-matching than the larger search-augmented Allie at a fraction of the parameters, which makes it attractive as a human-aligned policy with a tunable strength dial usable for win-probability scoring [8]. Monroe et al. also probe interpretability: a cross-layer transcoder on the middle layers surfaces features that localize to the squares of tactical motifs such as pins and forks, identified by manual inspection of top-activating positions. No ablation establishes that these features carry the model's move choice. They are exactly the correlational pin and fork "features" that a load-bearing test would have to confirm before an explanation could cite them [8]. Chessformer therefore sits on both sides of this survey's divide: a usable oracle, and at the same time a producer of the unverified concept claims the benchmark exists to grade.

Two works push imitation from the population down to the individual player. McIlroy-Young et al. fine-tune a base Maia model on a single player's game history, lifting move-matching roughly four to five points over the non-personalized baseline and enabling stylometry that identifies which of 400 players produced a set of moves with near-perfect accuracy from only a handful of games [49]. The method captures idiosyncratic, even error-prone decision patterns, but it needs thousands of games per player and remains a behavioral policy whose aggregate move-matching reveals nothing about which board factors drive a given prediction [49]. Maia4All attacks the data-scarcity barrier directly, fine-tuning Maia-2 on a skill-balanced set of prototype players and then learning only a per-player embedding for an unseen user, reaching comparable individual accuracy from far fewer games [48]. Its gains are reported purely as predictive fidelity, with no mechanistic or causal claim about why a player chooses a move [48]. Both sharpen whom a model represents without ever touching why a move is good.

Allie augments human-aligned imitation with a measured dose of search [10]. A transformer is trained on game logs to model not only move choices but human behaviors such as per-move thinking time and resignation, and at inference it runs a time-adaptive Monte-Carlo tree search whose budget scales with how long a human would deliberate, so the engine ponders at sharp positions rather than searching uniformly [10]. The adaptive variant tracks target strength closely across opponents rated 1000 to 2600, with an average skill gap near 49 Elo, and reaches grandmaster strength against the strongest players [10]. This makes Allie a candidate human-like oracle whose valuations are calibrated to a chosen rating, a property the benchmark can exploit. Its alignment, however, is measured behaviorally (move-match accuracy, Elo calibration, and timing) and carries no notion of explanation or reasoning, so it models what a human plays, not why, and supplies no decision-level causal ground truth [10].

A smaller body of work predicts human moves without large self-play networks, and is instructive precisely because it claims interpretability without verifying it. Skidanov et al. combine hand-engineered, human-meaningful features such as material, control, and threats with a behavior-based knowledge representation of how players actually decide, reporting a 25% relative gain in opening-phase move prediction for intermediate players and positioning concept features as more interpretable than opaque board evaluation [62]. That interpretability is asserted through the choice of human-meaningful features, never validated: nothing tests whether those features actually drive the predictions, which is the same unverified concept-based interpretability the benchmark sets out to close [62]. Zhong et al. go further toward lightweight modeling, training skill-group-specific n-gram language models over move sequences with a selector that classifies a game's skill group from early moves and routes it to the matching model [63]. The approach captures human variability cheaply, but it models surface move-token co-occurrence rather than board-state causal structure and offers no account of why a move is good [63]. Hamade et al. step outside prediction altogether: arguing that raw strength does not make an engine a good partner for a weaker player, they introduce skill-compatibility as a measurable trait separable from playing strength, and show skill-compatible agents outperforming stronger AlphaZero-based AI in collaborative settings despite being individually weaker [60]. The study targets human-AI interaction rather than explanation and offers no factor-level or causal analysis of any move [60].

Taken together, the human-aligned family delivers two things this survey needs and one it does not. It calibrates difficulty and audience (Maia per rating band [20], Maia-2 and Chessformer along a continuous skill axis [9], [8], and the individual-behavior models down to a single player [49], [48]), which fixes whom an explanation is for and how hard a position is for that reader. It supplies move-source baselines and candidate human-like oracles whose valuations are rating-calibrated, Allie among them [10], complementing the strength-maximizing engines that serve as the primary oracle the rest of the survey intervenes against. What it does not supply is an explanation: most of these models have no natural-language channel [9], [8], and those carrying concept-shaped internals expose them only correlationally [8], [62]. Human-likeness is not a causal account of which factors carry a decision, and predicting a human move is a different target from verifying that a cited factor is load-bearing for it. These systems are the substrate the benchmark scores against and the move sources it grades, not the explanation engines under test. The systems that do emit move-explanations, and the evaluations applied to them, are the subject of the next chapter.

## 8. Chess Explanation, Commentary & Grounded Reasoning

The systems surveyed in this chapter are the ones a causal-grounding benchmark would actually grade: engines and language models that emit move explanations citing concrete factors (a pin, a fork, a king-safety swing). Across two decades the output has grown from templated descriptions to motif-and-square reasoning traces, yet the evaluation attached to it has not kept pace. Every system below is scored by text quality, answer correctness, or internal self-consistency. None asks the question the survey's lens poses: was the cited factor causally load-bearing for the move, or merely name-dropped alongside it? This is the chapter that banks the explanation sources a grading benchmark would consume, and it shows the gap such a benchmark would close, since the most grounded systems here cite the very motifs (pins, forks, skewers) that a causal intervention can neutralize and re-measure.

Neural chess commentary set the template. Zang et al. couple a learned AlphaZero-style policy/value network to category-specific LSTM decoders, generating five commentary types (description, move quality, comparison, planning, and broader context) from the engine's internal state embedding and self-play rollouts [22]. The multi-task variant wins human evaluation with significance, but "accuracy" and "insight" are measured by BLEU/METEOR overlap and Likert ratings, and the win-rate signal that grounds the quality and comparison categories is produced by the same network, which makes those claims self-referential rather than validated against an independent oracle. Lee et al. push the grounding outward, steering a controllable BART model with tags drawn from a Leela engine: the win probability after the move, the optimal move, and a bucketed move-quality label [21]. Human raters prefer their commentary to earlier feature-based systems in roughly three-quarters of cases. The grounding remains coarse, offering win-probability buckets and optimal-move flags rather than the tactical motifs explanations actually cite, and the authors observe that crowdworkers sometimes disagree with the super-human engine, which undercuts preference as a measure of correctness. Both systems stop at behavioral and text-overlap proxies; neither intervenes to test a cited reason.

Concept conditioning is the closest the commentary literature comes to factor-level grounding. Kim et al. feed an LLM a prioritized list of human-interpretable concepts (read off engine internals by linear and SVM probes, then ranked by salience to the current decision) before it writes free-text prose, and pair this with GCC-Eval, a GPT-based judge validated against human raters [11]. The result is more accurate and fluent commentary under both human and LLM-judge assessment. Two limits matter for the survey's through-line. The concept vocabulary is coarse, consisting of positional evaluation terms a probe can recover, so tactical motifs such as pin, fork, and open file are not first-class, and the concept "prioritization" is a probe-delta ranking that shapes generation but is never tested for whether the highlighted concept is load-bearing for the move. The evaluation, moreover, is an LLM grading an LLM, inheriting that judge's biases, and the output is unstructured prose with no factor field to score.

Grounded reasoning models supply the cleanest explanation source a benchmark could ask for. Tang et al.'s C1 distills expert chess reasoning into a 4B-parameter model, using Stockfish at depth 24 to verify solutions during data construction and training through supervised fine-tuning plus a verifiable-reward RL stage [12]. C1 reaches 48.1% Pass@1 on its puzzle test set, surpassing far larger systems and its own distillation teacher while spending roughly two orders of magnitude fewer tokens, and its traces reference tactical themes (forks, pins, mating attacks) anchored to specific squares rather than emitting bare move predictions. The authors concede that the chain of thought "may not faithfully capture the underlying logic" on harder puzzles, naming the explainer-internal faithfulness gap directly. Their verification, however, reduces to move correctness: a right move paired with a wrong rationale still passes, because Stockfish certifies the solution, not the stated reason. C1 is therefore an ideal input to a grading benchmark, emitting native motif-with-square citations that the system itself flags as possibly unfaithful.

Two benchmarks try to measure chess understanding rather than generate commentary, and both grade answers instead of reasons. ChessQA organizes question-answering into five tiers of ascending difficulty (Structural, Motifs, Short Tactics, Position Judgment, and Semantic) and ships a refreshable, contamination-resistant harness; evaluated LLMs show persistent weaknesses across every tier [13]. As a QA benchmark it scores whether the answer is correct, with no intervention or counterfactual mechanism to test whether a model's cited factor is load-bearing for its decision. MATE takes the complementary tack, building a roughly one-million-position corpus in which expert annotators label five strategy categories and eight tactic types, including pin, fork, battery, and x-ray, then showing that a model fine-tuned with strategy-plus-tactic explanations selects the better of two candidate moves far more often than frontier commercial models [47]. The result demonstrates that explanations correlate with better move choice, but the headline gap conflates task specialization with reasoning, and nothing verifies that the verbalized factor is actually used rather than a post-hoc rationalization: the behavioral move-match stop-point again.

Neuro-symbolic agents add a verification layer, though one that closes on itself. Caïssa couples a fine-tuned LLM to a Prolog rule engine encoding tactics (forks, pins, skewers, discovered attacks, hanging pieces, mates) and a dynamically built knowledge graph, then runs a pipeline of small agents that decompose commentary into atomic statements and check each against the symbolic layer to suppress hallucinations [54]. It reports a 93.13% tactic-detection hit rate on Lichess puzzles. The verifier confirms consistency between the generated text and the system's own symbolic knowledge, the same source used to produce the commentary, so it cannot certify that a cited factor is causally load-bearing for the move's strength, and the authors concede the verifier itself can err. Its ground truth is self-consistency, not a decision-level causal effect.

Pedagogical work shows the substrate's promise from a different angle. Guid et al.'s endgame tutor compiles optimal play into a compact "conceptualized domain theory" of at most eleven production rules derived from tablebases, each encoding an intermediate goal reachable within a fixed depth, and a search engine grades each student move as correct, acceptable, or bad while flagging misconceptions [56]. It operationalizes chess concepts as verifiable goals checked against ground truth, an early demonstration that chess supplies decision-level structure for explanation, but it teaches against that structure rather than testing whether a cited factor drives a move, and it reports no empirical evaluation. At the outer edge, ASSESS reframes textbook move evaluation as aspect-based sentiment analysis, classifying an author's stance toward a move as positive, negative, or neutral over a small hand-annotated corpus, with Stockfish used loosely as a reference [61]. It measures rhetorical stance in prose, not game-theoretic correctness, and shares only the chess-plus-NLP-plus-engine setting.

SHAP-for-chess is the nearest prior art to an intervention-based grading of chess explanations, and the survey's framing extends it rather than displacing it. Spinnato adapts Shapley-value attribution by treating board pieces as features and a strong engine's evaluation as the value function: centipawn scores from Stockfish are mapped to a win probability by a logistic transform, and per-piece attributions are computed over coalitions of non-king pieces by ablating subsets and reading the resulting evaluation deltas, yielding additive, locally faithful, piece-by-piece explanations intended for pedagogy and position assessment [19]. This is precisely the move a board-level causal intervention makes: remove material, re-query the oracle, read the shift, established here as feasible and informative in chess, with worked examples spanning self-blocking pawns, trapped rooks, and pins. The survey's lens identifies three places its framing leaves room to build. The attribution is over static board occupancy, which piece sits where, not over the factors a move explanation cites, so it cannot isolate why a specific move is good or whether a named factor such as a pin is load-bearing. It offers only a board-level surface, and ablating a piece perturbs more than that piece's single property, a confound it does not separate out. And it advances no causal-grounding or statistical-testing framework: the author expressly cautions that a piece's Shapley value is not the evaluation change from removing it, and that the attributions should not guide move selection. These are extensions onto a respected precedent, not faults to dismiss: SHAP-for-chess proves the board-level reading is sound, and the survey's framing inherits it before adding a confound-free rule-level surface, a decision-level rather than position-level target, and the statistical apparatus that turns an evaluation delta into a graded claim.

Taken together, the chapter exposes a consistent shape. Chess explanation systems already emit factor-citing, often motif-and-square reasoning, yet they are graded by proxies that never reach decision-level causal load-bearing: text overlap [22], human preference [21], an LLM judge [11], move or answer correctness [12], [13], [47], symbolic self-consistency [54], pedagogical goal-checking [56], or rhetorical stance [61]. Only SHAP-for-chess [19] performs a board intervention, and it is the precedent the survey builds on. Explaining the move has a parallel in explaining the search that produces it, the subject of the next chapter.

## 9. Explaining Search & MCTS

A second branch of explainable AI targets the search process rather than a network's forward pass. Where the preceding chapters read explanations out of learned representations, the works here read them out of Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), the procedure that drives AlphaZero-style engines and general game-playing agents. The interpretive object is the post-search tree: visit counts, value estimates, principal variations, and the simulation statistics that accumulate at each node. Every method in this cluster surfaces an explanation from that structure. The question this survey poses to them is the one it poses elsewhere: does any verify that a surfaced statistic is causally load-bearing for the move actually chosen, or does it stop at describing the tree?

The earliest systematic framing comes from Baier and Kaisers [55], who assemble a toolset of building blocks for explaining MCTS decisions on Connect Four and Breakthrough. Every explanation derives from two post-search operations: tree simplification, which selects the principal variation and its siblings, and subtree summarization, which abstracts a subtree into the probabilities of pre-specified, domain-meaningful scenarios estimated from the proportion of simulations reaching them. The system also offers collaborative commands ("think about," "expect response," "search together") that re-run the search under user-supplied focus and report whether the decision changed, framing explanation as dialogue rather than one-way output. It is a position paper with no user study or metric, and its explanations remain correlational summaries of simulation proportions; nothing tests whether a summarized scenario is load-bearing. The collaborative re-search command is nonetheless the conceptual seed of a search-side intervention, since it perturbs the search and re-reads the outcome without yet making that perturbation a measured causal test.

Kowalski et al. [34] extend the descriptive approach while staying deliberately knowledge-free. Rather than encode game rules, they repurpose the internal statistics of standard MCTS enhancements as explanation material: MAST and its n-gram extension NST for context-free move values, GRAVE/AMAF for cross-phase estimates, Score-Bounded MCTS for pessimistic and optimistic terminal bounds, and proof-number search for the proof workload on a subtree. Built atop the Ludii general game-playing system, the proof-of-concept surfaces these quantities as mixed numeric-and-narrative explanations across six example positions, arguing they are richer than visit counts alone. The work is explicitly preliminary, with no user study, no quantitative explanation-quality metric, and no test that a surfaced statistic drives the chosen move. Its knowledge-free stance also holds explanations at the level of opaque counters and n-grams rather than human-meaningful tactics such as pins or hanging pieces, which are precisely the factors a move-level benchmark must grade.

A second strand pursues rigor by grounding explanations in formal structure. An et al. [36] translate user requirements into Computation Tree Logic (CTL) specifications via natural-language templates, model-check the MCTS tree against them, and render the verification results back into readable text. A study of 82 participants found the resulting explanations preferred over baselines. Their follow-up [37] couples the logic backbone to LLMs: an LLM classifies a query into one of 26 post-hoc types, few-shot prompting compiles it into typed logic statements, CTL model-checking and scorer functions extract evidence from the tree, and a QA model synthesizes an answer, sharply raising automated factual-consistency scores (FactCC, BERTScore) over raw GPT and Llama baselines on a paratransit-planning task. Qian et al. [38] take a different structural route, integrating shallow minimax into MCTS rollouts and then applying process mining to event logs of agent behavior, extracting process models that visualize the agents' decision strategies in 3v3 checkers.

These accounts source their evidence more rigorously than the descriptive readouts, yet their evaluation targets expose the same gap. An et al. measure user preference [36] and automated factual consistency against tree evidence [37]. Qian et al. visualize aggregate behavioral patterns [38]. Preference, factual consistency, and process visualization each certify that an explanation is grounded in the search, not that the cited factor is causally load-bearing for the decision the search produced.

Lu et al. [35] react against the brittleness of template- and logic-based pipelines with a template-free, LLM-driven explainer. The pipeline maps a natural-language question to a structured intent, checks whether the current tree holds enough evidence to answer it, and, when it does not, triggers targeted subtree expansion before generating an answer grounded in tree statistics. It handles node-, path-, and behavior-level questions, including contrastive and counterfactual queries. Evaluated only on FrozenLake with 21 hand-authored question-tree pairs and no baselines, its "grounding" is checked by a keyword heuristic the authors themselves call preliminary, a surface proxy rather than a causal test of which statistics drove the decision. The mechanism is nonetheless the most suggestive in the cluster: expanding a specific subtree to answer a specific question is, structurally, an intervention on the search itself.

Read together, this literature establishes the search tree as a legitimate explanation surface and builds up a vocabulary for operating on it: simplifying it [55], mining enhancement statistics from it [34], model-checking it [36], [37], abstracting it into process models [38], and expanding targeted subtrees of it [35]. What none supplies is a causal test of load-bearingness. Every method reads or grounds an explanation in the tree, and the two cases that come closest to perturbing the search, Baier and Kaisers' collaborative re-search [55] and Lu et al.'s targeted expansion [35], stop short of treating a decision delta as the explanation's warrant. This is the surface a later extension of factor-level causal grading could occupy. Intervening inside a search subtree, by pruning or forcing a branch and re-reading the agent's choice, would be complementary to the board- and rule-level factor interventions that are this survey's subject, not a competitor to them: one grades the reasons a position affords a strong oracle, the other would grade the reasons the search itself commits to. The MCTS-explanation literature is therefore best read here as a deferred intervention surface, broad in its grounding machinery and uniform in stopping one step short of the verified causal test.

## 10. Game-AI Explanation Beyond Chess

The explanation problem this survey traces is not native to chess. Card games pose the same two-part demand, a system that both acts well and says why, under the harder condition of hidden state, and two recent lines of work show each half emerging there on its own. Surveying them fixes the outer boundary of the survey's scope and tests whether its central move survives a change of game.

Tao et al. [57] build an LLM commentary generator for Guandan, a four-player imperfect-information card game. The system couples reinforcement-learning scenario generation with three language modules: a state guide that grounds narration in the current position, a Theory-of-Mind analyzer that reasons over opponents' concealed hands, and a style-retrieval component that imitates professional commentators. On open-source backbones it reportedly surpasses GPT-4 across several commentary-quality metrics. The output is exactly the kind of factor-citing narration the intervention lens is built to grade, naming the strategic reason a play is strong. Its evaluation stops at stylistic and fluency proxies, judging whether the commentary sounds expert rather than whether the cited factor actually drives the optimal play. The Theory-of-Mind reasoning is asserted rather than tested against the true hidden state, and the imperfect-information setting denies the system any clean oracle to intervene against. This is the stop-point Movement III met in every chess commentator: a rich explanation source with no test of causal load-bearing.

Wang et al. [58] ask the complementary question, whether LLMs can master complex card games as decisively as AlphaZero mastered chess and Go. They supervised-fine-tune models on strong-AI gameplay across eight card games and report three findings: fine-tuned LLMs approach specialized game-AI strength; a single model can learn several games at once, more readily when the games share rules; and deep game mastery erodes general instruction-following, a catastrophic-forgetting trade-off partly recovered by mixing general instruction data into training. The study concerns competence, not justification, and carries no move-explanation channel and no faithfulness or causal-intervention machinery. It matters here as the competence half without the explanation half: strong play that no one has yet asked to account for itself.

Read together, the two papers suggest—but, resting on only two works neither of which runs the intervention test, do not establish—that the survey's central move could generalize. The intervention-grounded lens, neutralize a cited factor and re-measure the decision, asks nothing chess-specific in principle, and it would apply to any decision a system can be made to take again under a controlled change. What chess supplies, and what Guandan does not, is the machinery that makes the re-measurement clean: rule-decidable factors and a strong, queryable oracle over fully observable state. Imperfect information removes the oracle and blurs the counterfactual, so card games can host the explanation problem but not yet its verifiable evaluation. On this thin two-paper base the generalization is a conjecture, not a demonstrated result; chess is therefore best read as a training ground for the method, not its endpoint. Once the recipe is validated where decision-level ground truth is computable, the open task is to carry it toward the harder, partially observable settings where these systems will eventually be asked to explain themselves.

## 11. Synthesis: where xai-chess fits

The three movements of this survey each deposited a premise. Movement I established that the faithfulness-evaluation literature converged on a mature recipe yet remains starved of verified labels; Movement II established that chess networks carry decidable, rule-grounded causal structure and admit strong oracles; Movement III established that real systems emit move-explanations and that the nearest prior art grades them only at the board level. This chapter spends those premises. It states the project's five-part thesis against the surveyed evidence and, as the only chapter licensed to do so, reports xai-chess's own results. The compressed claim is that the method is settled, the binding constraint is ground truth, two ground-truth targets must be kept apart, and chess is the substrate that supplies the verifiable one.

### 11.1 The method is settled; the bottleneck is ground truth

The chain-of-thought community has already converged, by independent reinvention, on the evaluation recipe xai-chess borrows: neutralize a cited factor with a matched baseline and test statistically whether the decision moves. The recipe instances are Lanham [29], RFEval [16], and ICE [17], with Turpin's planted-bias input interventions [28] as the input-side proto-instance and Jacovi's definition of the target [30] behind them all. ICE [17] supplies the full statistical stack, a win-rate primary metric with Cohen's *d*, bootstrap confidence intervals, and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control, reported comparatively across intervention operators. RFEval [16] arrives at counterfactual reasoning interventions and the conclusion that accuracy is no proxy for faithfulness. A second tier of meta-evaluators sits one level up, grading the metrics themselves rather than instantiating the recipe: Causal Diagnosticity [15] scores metrics on edit-based counterfactual pairs, BonaFide [14] meta-evaluates a whole suite of perturbation metrics against ground truth, and BUMP [44] does the same over unfaithful minimal pairs. When several teams redundantly rediscover one pattern, that pattern is shared field property, not a contribution to be claimed. Here the borrowing must be stated precisely, because it sharpens rather than blurs the project's own thesis. xai-chess adopts the *statistical apparatus* wholesale—matched baseline, randomization test, win rate, Cohen's *d*, bootstrap CI, BH-FDR, multi-operator reporting—but redirects it to a *different estimand*. The recipe instances above are target-1 instruments: they intervene on the explainer's own reasoning or inputs and read the explainer's own decision, estimating self-faithfulness on the system under test. xai-chess instead intervenes on the world—the board or the rules—and reads a *separate oracle's* decision, estimating decision-level causal load-bearing (the second target). The explainer whose claim is graded and the oracle intervened upon are different systems, so xai-chess grades reason-correctness against an oracle, not faithfulness to any explainer. Granting the shared apparatus is what isolates the real contribution.

That mature recipe is universally short of verified labels. BonaFide [14] builds a costly labeling pipeline and still finds its best chain-of-thought-level metric reaches only 0.70 AUROC, with most metrics near chance. Causal Diagnosticity [15] manufactures edit-based labels that go unverified, and its own analysis reports that models often ignore the edit, forcing a fall-back to synthetic explanations. ICE [17] substitutes randomized matched baselines precisely because per-item labels do not exist. The 0.70 ceiling under hand-curated labels shows the limiting factor is the labels themselves, not the metric arithmetic. Throughout this synthesis the 0.70 figure serves as a reference bar to clear, not as evidence of a failed field.

### 11.2 Two ground-truth targets must not be conflated

"Faithfulness ground truth" silently names two different things, and the survey's central conceptual move is to separate them. The first is explainer-internal faithfulness: whether an explanation reflects the explainer's own computation. Jacovi [30] fixes this as the field's stated target and distinguishes it from mere plausibility. The second is decision-level causal load-bearing: whether the cited factor is actually doing causal work for a competent decision. Sandmann [5] proves the two come apart, showing that a chess network's internal solutions, including found mates, are systematically overridden by later layers before output, so internal computation and emitted behavior can legitimately diverge. McGrath [1] then shows that the chess-native instrument, concept probing, is explicitly correlational and names the pin / can_capture_queen confound: a concept can be decodable without being causally used. Probing therefore does not deliver the first target, and only intervention can certify the second.

This disambiguation forces a lexical discipline that doubles as the answer to the strongest objection against the project, that measuring something other than the explainer's internals makes the word "faithfulness" a category error. The project concedes the word. It does not claim to read the graded commentator's unobservable internals; it claims only that each cited factor is causally load-bearing for a strong oracle's decision, a grounding and correctness property complementary to internal faithfulness rather than a replacement for it. Because the two targets are genuinely distinct, naming the measured property "faithfulness" would itself be the error. Declining the term is the rigorous choice, and the project lives entirely on the decision-level side of the distinction.

### 11.3 Chess as the substrate

Chess co-locates three properties that text cannot supply together, which is what lets the intervention recipe finally run against a verifiable label. Factor presence is decidable by the rules, so detection via python-chess is exact by construction. Decision quality is read from a strong oracle. The empirical results reported in §11.5 use Stockfish 18 at a fixed 400k-node, single-thread budget, which is deterministic, as the oracle that supplies the per-position valuation the win-probability and log-odds scales are read from. A searchless action-value head such as Ruoss's amortized planner [7] is an attractive *candidate alternative* oracle—it yields a calibrated, search-free per-move win-probability that avoids any centipawn-to-probability conversion—but it is discussed here from the literature and did not produce the numbers below. The causal structure each factor implicates is independently attested as real and testable inside chess networks (Ch. 6; e.g., [4], [6]). McGrath's pin / can_capture_queen confound [1] is exactly the correlational ambiguity that a causal intervention dissolves, and chess hands the analyst the precise factor to neutralize. SHAP-for-chess [19] further demonstrates that intervening on the board and reading an evaluation delta is feasible in this domain. The ground truth is the causal fact, whether suspending the pin changes the best continuation under the rules, made legible by the oracle rather than reducible to the oracle's preference. No other surveyed substrate brings decidable detection, a clean win-probability oracle, and attested causal structure into one place, which is why chess is the cleanest venue for the decision-level target, not merely one venue among many.

### 11.4 Novelty, scoped honestly

The contribution, stated narrowly, is not non-text-ness as such—prior causal interventions already operate on non-text substrates—but a confound-free rule-level intervention surface together with a measurable agreement between intervention surfaces, both graded against a separate oracle with win-probability deltas as treatment effects. The claim is established by differencing against the nearest neighbors, which must be named as competing "firsts" rather than mere templates. Atrey [32] already performs state-level, environment-grounded counterfactual interventions on a non-text RL substrate; what it lacks is a separate decision oracle, the statistical intervention-consistency layer, and the move-factor target, so it is the closest non-text precedent the scoped claim must be differenced from rather than a counterexample to it. SHAP-for-chess [19] has the board-level remove-a-piece move but attributes static board occupancy rather than move-explanation factors, evaluates qualitatively over seven illustrative positions, and offers no confound-free surface, no agreement test, and no ground-truth framing. Read against these two, the precise and defensible claim is the conjunction—oracle-backed AND statistical intervention-consistency AND a confound-free rule-level surface AND measurable multi-surface agreement on a move-factor target—not a bare "first on a non-text substrate." ICE [17] supplies the precedent that operator-comparative reporting is a valued output rather than a defect. Causal Diagnosticity [15] supplies the thing to improve on: grade behavior against an oracle instead of against an unverified edit. A win-probability oracle is what makes "treatment effect" well defined in the first place—Stockfish's evaluation in the reported results (§11.3), with a searchless value head such as Ruoss's [7] as a candidate alternative.

The recurring objection is that this is "just SHAP-for-chess with extra steps," and the rebuttal is to show the extra step changes the verdict. The board-level surface relocates a piece, which carries a confound: moving the pinned-to king off the ray also perturbs king-safety, so it moves more than the one property under test. The rule-level surface, a roughly twenty-line movegen patch to Stockfish exposed as an opt-in `MaskPinner` UCI option (the patched build `stockfish-xai-rulelevel`; with the option off it is byte-identical to stock), instead suspends the pin in the rules with no piece moved, letting the pinned piece move while denying the pinning slider any check or capture along the ray. A stock engine cannot reproduce this without literally exposing the king, which would invert the signal. "Confound-free" must be qualified honestly, however: the rule-level surface removes the board-relocation confound but trades it for a possible engine-modification confound, since the patched search now explores positions illegal under standard rules and could in principle perturb the evaluation landscape in ways unrelated to the pin. Two pieces of evidence suggest the trade is benign—the `MaskPinner` default leaves the build byte-identical to stock, so non-pin behavior is unchanged whenever the option is off, and the rule-level surface agrees with the board-level surface on every golden pin case (§11.5)—but a dedicated null control (patched-vs-stock evaluation on pin-free positions) is left to future work, so the defensible claim is "free of the board-relocation confound," not free of all confounds. The relocation-confound-free surface and the agreement test are the differentiators, and the measured gap between surfaces, reported next, is what proves the added step is decisive rather than decorative.

### 11.5 The project's empirical evidence

What follows is the survey's one embedded original contribution, reported as such: these are the authors' own results, produced by the open xai-chess repository and pending external validation, not third-party evidence surveyed from the literature. The reproducibility basics are stated up front. The oracle is Stockfish 18 at a fixed 400k-node, single-thread budget, which is deterministic; the rule-level surface uses the movegen-patched build `stockfish-xai-rulelevel` (opt-in `MaskPinner`, default byte-identical to stock). The corpus is the Lichess puzzle database (CC0), N = 164 (83 pin-themed "faithful" / 81 incidental-pin "red herring"), with labels assigned by Lichess tactic themes and therefore independent of the metric under test. The pipeline is open and reproducible from the repository: `lichess_ingest.py` builds the JSONL corpus, `corpus_eval.py` computes AUROC with bootstrap confidence intervals, and `baselines.py` supplies the random and saliency baselines.

The apparatus is a factor-typed DSL in which each factor type supplies `detect`, `to_nl`, and `remove`. Two types are implemented: `pin`, which admits both surfaces, and `hanging`, which is board-level only because nothing "hanging" is a rule of chess to suspend. Outcomes are read on three scales. Win-probability change, eff_wp, is bounded and interpretable but saturates near won or lost positions; log-odds change, eff_logit, de-saturates the tails and is the robust default; centipawn change, eff_cp, never saturates but is noisy at the extremes. On six golden cases (three pin, three hanging), load-bearing citations and red herrings separate cleanly at eff_wp ≥ 0.10, and the two surfaces agree on every pin case. A saturated but genuinely load-bearing position exposes the eff_wp ceiling, registering a false "red herring" at +0.078 that eff_cp ≈ +197cp and eff_logit ≈ +0.73 recover, and the `hanging` quarantine path fires correctly when the cited piece is in fact defended.

The Lichess meta-evaluation uses CC0 puzzles with labels independent of the metric, taking `pin`-themed puzzles as the load-bearing (positive) class and incidental-pin non-`pin` puzzles as red herrings, with N = 164 (83 positive, 81 red herring) scored over 400k Stockfish-18 oracle nodes (§11.3), all scorers run on the same records.

The rule-level interval lies entirely above the 0.70 BonaFide reference bar [14], the only scorer that clears it. Paired bootstraps confirm the ordering is not noise: rule minus saliency is +0.21 [+0.12, +0.30] and rule minus board is +0.13 [+0.04, +0.23], both excluding zero. The headline follows directly: which intervention surface the analyst uses decides whether the metric works at all, a contrast visible only because the causal effect is computable, which is exactly the affordance chess supplies.

Two hazards must be named where the evidence is reported. The first is circularity: a metric is worthless as its own label. A forced-mate "verified-label" probe is genuinely circular for the rule-level metric, because the label and the metric share the same counterfactual of suspending the pin, so that probe is retired and reported only as a cautionary artifact. The Lichess evaluation avoids the trap by sourcing its label exogenously from the corpus's own theme tag, computed by no intervention. The second is thinness: a single factor, a single oracle, and a noisy proxy tag. This is conceded as scope. The defensible result is the contrast, not the absolute number, and the rule ≫ board ≫ saliency ordering with paired intervals excluding zero is robust to label noise even where the absolute AUROC is measured against a proxy.

### 11.6 Surface disagreement is informative, not a bug

A natural worry is that an oracle will score a perfectly sound human explanation as non-load-bearing on a position that is already won. The reframe is that this is a measurement-scale effect, not a verdict on the human. On a won position the win-probability is saturated, so neutralizing even a genuinely cited factor barely moves eff_wp, which is precisely the saturated load-bearing golden case, and the de-saturating scales eff_cp and eff_logit recover the true signal. The broader point is that metric-human divergence is expected. ICE [17] finds operator gaps up to 44 percentage points and essentially zero correlation between faithfulness and human plausibility (|r| < 0.04), and Sandmann [5] supplies the mechanism, since internal solutions found and then discarded mean activation-level and behavioral interventions can legitimately disagree. Disagreement across surfaces, scales, or against human judgment is a finding the substrate makes legible, and surfacing it is part of the contribution.

## 12. Limitations of the surveyed field and open problems

The project's own boundaries are worth stating plainly, because they recur across the field rather than being peculiar to it. The Lichess `pin` tag is an independent but noisy proxy: the rule-versus-baseline contrasts are robust to that noise, yet the absolute AUROC is measured against a proxy and not against verified ground truth. Only random and saliency are ported as same-task baselines, because BonaFide's other six metrics [14] are chain-of-thought-specific with no chess analogue, which makes the 0.70 figure a cross-domain reference bar rather than a head-to-head competitor. The demonstration rests on one factor type and one oracle. The forced-mate "verified-label" probe is circular for the rule-level metric, since label and metric share the counterfactual, and has been retired; catching and removing a circular item is a sign of rigor, not a wound.

These boundaries mirror weaknesses that run through the surveyed corpus. Labels are proxies almost everywhere, whether unverified edits [15], plausibility-bound simulatability scores, or noisy human tags, and the field rarely confirms that the entity supposedly under test was the entity the model actually used. Interpretability repeatedly stops at correlation, beginning with the canonical chess probing work [1] and its successors, which establish decodability without establishing causal use, so a high probe accuracy proves a representation is present, never that it is load-bearing. Mechanistic surfaces are fragile under net-binding: the strongest internal causal results are pinned to exact network checkpoints (Leela in [4], BT4 in [6], and a separate action-value transformer in [7]), and the relevant features cannot always be ablated inside the host engine, so a mechanistic intervention is only as portable as the one checkpoint it was validated on. Single deterministic verdicts without a randomization layer also persist, as in RFEval's single indicator [16], leaving the field without a principled null against which "no effect" can be read.

The agenda these gaps imply is concrete. First, surface-agreement deserves promotion from an internal robustness check to a primary reported result. ICE [17] already treats operator gaps of up to 44 percentage points as informative rather than as noise to be averaged away, and Sandmann [5] supplies the mechanism for why surfaces should be expected to diverge, since computation that is found and then discarded means board-level, rule-level, and any future activation-level intervention need not agree. A benchmark that reports cross-surface agreement as a first-class quantity, with its own confidence intervals, would convert a sanity check into evidence about where and why explanations and oracles part company. Second, a third, mechanistic intervention surface, internal feature ablation in the style of the look-ahead and transcoder work, could join the board-level and rule-level surfaces, but only if it is bound to one exact network hash, since the net-binding fragility above makes any mechanistic claim checkpoint-relative. Third, the factor DSL is presently hand-authored, with `pin` and `hanging` written by hand; extracting cited factors automatically from free-text commentary (Ch. 8; e.g., [12]) is open and is what would let the benchmark grade real explanation sources at scale rather than curated cases. Fourth, evaluation should be stratified by position difficulty, since the saturation behavior reported above shows that effect sizes are not comparable across won, drawn, and sharp positions; the human-difficulty calibrators surveyed in Ch. 7 are the natural instrument for binning positions before scoring. Finally, whether the methodology transfers beyond chess is at present a conjecture, not a result: it rests on only two cross-domain works ([57], [58]), neither of which actually runs the intervention test. The methodology should transfer wherever a domain offers both decidable factors and a strong oracle, so porting the rule-versus-board surface-agreement test beyond chess, toward the imperfect-information card-game settings at the survey's outer boundary, is the proper—and still unrun—test of whether the contribution is chess-specific or a general template for grading the reasons behind a decision.

## 13. Conclusion

The faithfulness-evaluation field has converged on a mature method and is now limited by something the method cannot provide. Neutralize a cited factor with a matched baseline and test statistically whether the decision moves: ICE [17], RFEval [16], Causal Diagnosticity [15], and BonaFide [14] all instantiate this recipe, which is therefore shared property rather than any single project's novelty. What every instantiation lacks is verified ground truth, and BonaFide's 0.70 ceiling under costly hand labels [14] marks the bottleneck precisely. The same shortage shows up at both ends of the spectrum, in the unverified edit labels of Causal Diagnosticity [15] and in the randomized baselines ICE [17] adopts because per-item labels do not exist, which is why the constraint is now the substrate rather than the statistics.

The survey's organizing distinction is that two ground-truth targets have been conflated. Explainer-internal faithfulness, whether an explanation reflects the explainer's own computation [30], is genuinely separable from decision-level causal load-bearing, whether a cited factor does causal work, and Sandmann's evidence that internal solutions are overridden before output [5] shows the two can come apart. Chess supplies the second target verifiably, by combining rule-decidable factor detection, a strong win-probability oracle (Stockfish for the reported results, with a searchless value head [7] as a candidate alternative), and attested rule-grounded causal structure, a combination no other surveyed substrate offers.

On that substrate, xai-chess contributes a confound-free rule-level intervention surface and a measurable agreement between intervention surfaces, graded against a separate oracle with win-probability deltas as treatment effects. The scoped novelty is that conjunction, differenced from the nearest non-text precedent, Atrey's state-level RL counterfactuals [32], which lack a separate oracle and the statistical layer, and from SHAP-for-chess [19], which has only the board-level surface and none of the framing—not a bare claim of being first on a non-text substrate. The decisive lesson, reported here as the authors' own result pending external validation rather than as surveyed third-party evidence, is that intervention-surface choice determines whether the metric works, with the confound-free rule-level surface clearing the reference bar that board-level and saliency scorers do not. That result is not a chess curiosity; it is a warning that any intervention-based evaluation can pass or fail on the operator alone, which is invisible in domains where no computable causal effect lets the surfaces be compared.

The contribution is thus a validated substrate and a grounding property that complements, and does not replace, explainer-internal faithfulness. The scope is honest about its limits, a single factor, a single oracle, and a noisy proxy tag, yet the contrast it demonstrates is robust where the absolute numbers are not. For a field that has the method but not the labels, chess offers a setting in which the recipe can finally be tested against ground truth it cannot otherwise obtain, and in which the gap between a plausible reason and a load-bearing one becomes a quantity rather than a hope.
